# Catalog and build order

> Which components exist, in what order they are built, and what has been
> ruled out.
> **This is not a foundation.** Foundations are permanent rules; this is a
> living list that changes weekly. They are kept apart so the foundations do
> not look less stable than they are.

**Status:** a chosen list, not a queue · **Date:** 2026-09-09

---

## 1. A warning about this list

The list below is a **forecast**, based on what nearly every management
application needs. It is not a commitment, and it is not a queue that has to be
worked through in order.

Which of these get built, and in what order, is a deliberate decision
([decision 0008](./decisions/0008-the-rule-of-two-splits.md)). Real screens
change that decision when they exist — that is the point of having a consumer —
but their absence does not block it.

## 2. How a component gets in

1. It is a **component**, not a prop on one that already exists. If the need
   can be met by an existing component, that is the cheaper answer — and the
   prop it would take faces the stricter half of P5
2. It passes the **thirteen checkboxes** of the entry gate
   ([doc 01](./foundations/01-principles.md)). Thirteen of thirteen, not twelve
3. It breaks no non-goal

No component enters half-built. "Since we're already here" is still the reason
things rot — it just rots through props rather than through the catalog.

## 3. How the order is decided

Three criteria, in strict priority:

**1. Technical dependency.** Overrides everything else. You cannot build a
select without having the field and the layers first.

**2. Real demand, when it exists.** Within what criterion 1 permits, real
screens dictate the order over this list. With no consumer yet, that criterion
is quiet and the order falls to criteria 1 and 3 — which is why the bottlenecks
and the risky piece come first.

**3. Risk, once.** A difficult component is built **early**, even if nobody has
asked for it: if the composition model does not hold, it is far better to find
out with five components built than with twenty.

### 3.1 The batch after the layers, and why it is split in two

Recorded because the shape of it is not obvious, and because the reason is
criterion 1 above rather than anybody's preference.

Six components were proposed together: `Tabs`, a stepper, `Breadcrumbs`,
`Pagination`, `CursorPagination` and an accordion. Four of them want something
that does not exist — `Select` for a narrow `Tabs` and for a page size, `Menu`
for a collapsed breadcrumb trail, and the structural-change hook of
[doc 04](./foundations/04-responsive.md) §6 for three of them. So the batch is
delivered in two halves, with the missing pieces in between:

| Part            | What lands                                                                                                                                                 | Why in this order                                                                                                                           |
| --------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **First half**  | The chevron, `Accordion` and `Collapsible`, `Link`, `Breadcrumbs` without the collapse, then the §6 hook together with `Pagination` and `CursorPagination` | Nothing here is blocked. The hook arrives with its first real caller rather than on its own, because a hook nobody calls cannot be verified |
| **In between**  | `Menu` and `Select`                                                                                                                                        | Three deferred features wait on exactly these two, and `SplitButton` has been waiting on `Menu` since the layer batch                       |
| **Second half** | `Tabs` in full and the breadcrumb collapse. ~~A page size~~ — asked and answered once `Select` existed: it belongs to the listing, not to the pager        | The features that cannot be built honestly today, built once                                                                                |

**A stepper is in neither half.** It is two components sharing one name, and the
half worth building is `Steps`, beside `Progress`
([decision 0015](./decisions/0015-a-stepper-is-two-components.md)).

The alternative was to build `Tabs` now with a scrolling tab list and swap the
select in later. It was rejected: the second pass changes the component's
structure, so everything already verified is verified again — and doc 04 §6
rule 4, that state survives the structural change, is exactly the kind of
guarantee that is cheap to design in and expensive to retrofit.

### 3.2 The batch after composition, and why it is six waves

**Date:** 2026-09-09. Recorded before the work starts, which is what §8 asks of
every decision to build something.

Thirteen pieces were proposed together, read off the inventory of a working
management application: a searchable select, an asynchronous one, four date and
time controls, four catalogue fields, a phone field, a discount field, a colour
picker, a file uploader, a signature pad and a rich text editor. Read against
this list it produced four answers rather than one batch, because a list like
that mixes four different kinds of thing:

| What was proposed            | What it turned out to be                              |
| ---------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| `ComboBox` and the dates     | Components, and the ones this batch builds            |
| The asynchronous combo box   | Not a component: a hook and a `ComboBox` (P6)         |
| The four catalogue fields    | Data — and three of the four need none, see §7        |
| Everything asked of `Select` | Props on a component that exists, so P5's strict half |

**The order inside the batch is criteria 1 and 3 again**, not preference.
`ComboBox` is the risk component and goes first, for the reason §6 gives. The
dates then go in dependency order: a calendar before the picker that opens one,
and a segmented field before the picker that contains one, because both are
built either way and a screen showing a calendar inline is a real screen rather
than a by-product of a popover.

| Wave   | What lands                            | Why here                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| ------ | ------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **0**  | The two lines this batch needed first | Doc 07 §2.2 gained a seventh contender for a field's trailing edge, and a date's public value is now settled (0020)                                                                                 |
| **1**  | `ComboBox`, one value                 | The risk component (§6). Nothing it depends on is missing                                                                                                                                           |
| **2**  | `ComboBox`, several values            | A second shape of value, and the tags it shows in a 320px container are their own problem (P4)                                                                                                      |
| **3**  | `useAsyncOptions`                     | Paging and server-side filtering, over the base's `useAsyncList`. A hook, because P6's corollary asks for one                                                                                       |
| **4**  | `Calendar`                            | The picker's content and a component on its own terms. The three chained views land here                                                                                                            |
| **4b** | `RangeCalendar`                       | Split out of wave 4 after building it, and it earned the split: two synchronised months, a range's own selection painting, and the first structural change in a component sized by its own contents |
| **5**  | `DateField` and `DatePicker`          | Typing a date is the picker's own keyboard route, so the field is built either way — and it is the wave that found doc 07 §2.2's one exception                                                      |
| **6**  | `TimeField` and `DateRangePicker`     | The two that close the family — and the wave that gave doc 04 §5's exception its first caller                                                                                                       |

**Three levels on purpose.** A calendar depends on nothing, a date field
depends on `Field`, and a picker depends on both of those plus a layer — so
this batch spans levels 1, 2 and 4, and the order above is the dependency
rather than the level number.

**Seven waves rather than six**, and the extra one is recorded here rather than
absorbed: wave 4 was planned as both calendars and `Calendar` alone filled it.
It arrived with a declared dependency, an amendment to one of the project's own
lint rules, a shared internal for parsing dates and a decision about which day
is today — and a range calendar adds two synchronised months, a different
selection to paint and a structure that changes with its container. Two PRs
that can each be read beat one that cannot.

**What is deliberately not in it.** The catalogue fields are pure functions
rather than components, the phone field is the first runtime dependency this
library would take, the colour picker and the uploader are sequencing, the
signature pad is blocked on a line doc 06 does not have yet, and the editor is
still waiting for a project that needs it. Each has its row in §7 with the
reason. **F8 has no leftovers left.** `Progress` turned out to be a dependency
rather than a leftover — a file uploader shows progress per file — `Steps`
landed beside it, which is where a stepper's reporting half was always going to
go ([decision 0015](./decisions/0015-a-stepper-is-two-components.md)), and
`ButtonGroup` closed the phase.

### 3.3 The three that were only ever sequencing

**Date:** 2026-09-10. Recorded before the work starts, which is what §8 asks.

Three rows of §7 said "sequencing only" rather than giving a reason of
substance, and the sequence has arrived: the layer base exists, `Progress`
exists, and `Slider` exists. They are `Avatar`, the colour controls and the
file uploader.

**The order is criterion 1 and nothing else.** `Avatar` goes first, and not
because it is the smallest: it shares a wall with the uploader and nothing else
shares it — **a box holding an image that may not arrive, with something in its
place.** An avatar falls back to initials and a thumbnail falls back to a glyph
for the file's type, and the mechanism underneath is the same. Built in that
order, the uploader is the second caller and the extraction into `internal/`
happens where §8's own rule puts it, with the baselines coming out identical as
the proof. Built the other way it is written twice, and the cross reached four
copies that way.

The uploader goes last because it has the most doctrine to settle and is the
one piece that might turn out not to be a component.

| Wave | What               | Why there                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| ---- | ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1    | `Avatar`           | Level 1, depends on nothing, and settles the image-with-a-fallback the uploader needs                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| 2    | `ColorSwatchField` | **Done.** The cheap half of the colour pair, by its own row. It settled the VALUE's boundary with the least machinery around it — [decision 0024](./decisions/0024-a-colour-crosses-as-a-string-and-the-format-is-declared.md), written first                                          |
| 3    | `ColorPicker`      | **Done.** Area and sliders in a layer, on the boundary wave 2 settled. Exactly `DatePicker`'s shape, because the base publishes a colour state the same way — and it is where decision 0024's `format` prop earns its keep: a value dragged out of an area was never one of the inputs |
| 4    | `FileUpload`       | **Done, in ONE wave.** It was planned as two — the zone and the list, then the restrictions and the errors — and the second half came to four props and no new machinery. It is also where "a retry button inside a loaded list" got its answer, in a narrower form than the row asks  |

**Three things are settled before any of it is written**, because a foundation
changes before the code and never afterwards to justify it:

1. **Initials are not derived from a name.** Doc 05 §4.2, written with this
   batch: turning "Carlos Ramos" into "CR" is a locale-dependent
   transformation, and there are languages where it produces nothing. The
   fallback arrives as children.
2. **A colour crosses as a string, in the format it arrived in.** Decision
   0020's shape on a third type after days and times, and it needs its own
   record rather than a line here.
3. **The uploader does not upload.** Hard rule 6 allows it no request at all,
   so it receives files and reports them and the progress per file is a prop —
   the arrangement `useToasts` has, where the consumer owns the queue.

**And the plan was wrong about the shape of the second half.** Two waves were
budgeted because "the restrictions and the errors" sounded like a body of work.
Measured against what a library may actually do: a restriction is `accept`,
which narrows what the file dialog offers the way `maxLength` narrows what can
be typed, and **every error is text the project wrote** — too big, the wrong
type, a request that came back 500 are all judgements a component that never
sent anything cannot make (doc 07 §1). So the second wave was `accept`,
`allowsMultiple`, a per-row `errorMessage` and a per-row retry, and splitting
the PR would have split one component's props across two reviews.

What the wave did cost was an INSTRUMENT rather than a design. A drop built in
the page delivers no file — `webkitGetAsEntry()` answers null for an item that
did not come from a real drag, and the base skips such an item — so the drop
is driven through the DevTools protocol with real paths on disk
([doc 10](./foundations/10-quality-and-verification.md) §11.1). The check that
looked finished first was green and proved nothing.

**And the retry landed because the list is not a collection.** §7 keeps "a
retry button inside a loaded list" pending a measurement, and that row is about
a row of a LISTBOX, whose own keyboard cannot reach a button inside it. An
uploader's rows are a plain `<ul>` — nothing here is a collection, because
nothing here is chosen — so a button in a row is just a button, and the retry
appears on a row that failed AND only where a consumer passed something that
can service it. The pending measurement is untouched.

### 3.4 The table suite, and how many pieces it is

**Date:** 2026-09-11. Recorded before the work starts, which is what §8 asks.

The catalog carries **one row** for the whole suite — "Table pieces: header,
row, cell, sorting, selection **plus their hooks** (P6). Not a closed table" —
and one row is exactly where a suite goes wrong. Non-goal 4 is unusually
explicit that the functionality is first-class and that what is forbidden is
_how_: "one component that does everything through accumulated props". So the
piece list is written here, before the first wave, and the temptation it exists
to resist is naming this section's contents as props on a large `Table`.

**It was read against a real one first.** A hand-written data table from a
management product, around twenty thousand lines, with the features its users
had asked for over several years — inventoried at 145 capabilities, then
checked against these documents and against what the base already provides.
Three of its ideas are carried across, one of its shapes is refused, and the
rest is either already ours or already the base's. The refusal is the useful
part and it is in §7's own row for it.

#### What the base already does, which is most of it

Measured at the pinned versions rather than assumed. `Table`, `TableHeader`,
`TableBody`, `TableFooter`, `Column`, `ColumnResizer`, `Row`, `Cell`,
`TableLoadMoreItem` and `ResizableTableContainer` are all real, and so are
**sorting** (the descriptor, `aria-sort`, and the announcement in 34 locales),
**selection** (shift-ranges from pointer AND keyboard, `Mod+A`, an
indeterminate select-all with a localized label), **grid keyboard navigation**
with typeahead, **column resizing** as a real `input[type=range]` with keyboard
and localized pixel announcements, **row drag and drop** with keyboard and
screen-reader sessions, **expandable rows**, and **column spans in both
directions**.

That is the list the suite must not rewrite (non-goals 6 and 7). It also means
most of the accessibility defects a hand-written table accumulates do not need
fixing here — they do not arise.

**What the base does NOT have**, and is therefore ours or the project's:
pagination (two components, already built, and they stay separate — decision
0014), column reordering, column visibility, a sticky header outside
virtualization, computed totals (`TableFooter` is markup only), row density,
and persistence of anything.

#### The pieces

| Kind         | What                                                                                          |
| ------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Hooks**    | the column state — visibility, order, width — and the paging state, which owns the rule below |
| **Pieces**   | our skin on the base's set, plus the resizer                                                  |
| **Assembly** | one, thin, adding **nothing**                                                                 |

Three things about that table are decisions rather than shape.

**There is no selection hook.** The base's selection is complete, including the
parts people reimplement, so a hook of ours would be a wrapper — and doc 02 §5
already says internal state comes out as a named hook only where there is state
worth reaching. Wrapping someone else's complete state is not that.

**The column hook holds the state and stores nothing.** P3 is not negotiable
and is enforced by lint: the library does not write to `localStorage`. So
"remember my columns" is the PROJECT's, and the hook is what makes it possible
— it receives the arrangement and reports changes, the shape `useToasts` set.
This also repairs a defect measured in the product this was read against, where
preferences fell back to a per-path local key whenever the provider was not
mounted, so in tests and in half the screens they never reached the server at
all.

**The paging hook owns one rule that nobody writes by hand**, and it is the
substance of §7's re-asked page-size row: when the page size changes, the
page is re-anchored on the first row that was visible. 200 results at 10 a
page, on page 7, switching to 50: keep the page and you are on rows 301 to 350,
past the end, looking at nothing. Anchor on row 61 and page 2 of 50 still holds
it.

#### The test the assembly has to pass

P6's corollary, applied literally at the end of the last wave: **can the
assembly be rebuilt from the public pieces, losing nothing?** If a capability
is reachable only by passing a prop to the assembly, the model is wrong and
that capability belongs in a hook. The precedent is already paid for — the
asynchronous combo box is a hook for exactly this reason.

#### Three measurements before the API, not after

Each of these has already cost this repository once in a different component,
and each is a browser question rather than a reasoning one.

1. **One context per collection.** A table is collections inside collections,
   and decision 0022 measured what that does: a `TagGroup` inside a `ComboBox`
   resolves the wrong collection and either exhausts the heap or throws, and
   every `Button` in the subtree wears the toggle's props. The first row with a
   chip or a button in it meets this.
2. **The base THROWS rather than warns** on a cell count that does not match
   the column count — measured, and the message is
   `Cell count must match column count. Found 1 cells and 2 columns.` Any API
   on top has to make that true by construction rather than hope for it.
   ~~exactly one column must be `isRowHeader`~~ — **measured false**, and what
   is there instead is worse; see the wave 0 findings below.
3. **Resizing imposes inline styles our CSS cannot beat** — `table-layout:
fixed` and a pixel `width` per `th`. "The table fills its container" and
   "the columns resize" are in tension, and so is anything sticky. **Measured
   in wave 0, and the tension is real in one half and imaginary in the other.**

**Three things the base offers that are deliberately NOT waves**: expandable
rows, grouped headers and incremental loading. Non-goal 4's list does not name

one decides it.

#### What wave 0 measured

**Date:** 2026-09-11. Three questions were asked; seven things came back, and
one of them corrects the paragraph above.

**A button inside a cell does not rename the row.** This is the opposite of the
measured `ListBox` case, where a row came back announced as
`option "A row with a button Retry"`. Measured here: the row is named **exactly
`"Ana"`** — a query for `"AnaEdit"` matches nothing — and the button keeps its
own name, its own generated id, and no slot. So the §7 row about a retry button
inside a loaded list keeps its answer and gains its boundary: **the test is
whether the collection gives a keyboard route to the control, and a grid does
where a listbox does not.** Row actions are legitimate here.

**The missing row header does not throw. It goes silent, which is worse.**
Measured in three configurations — plain, with `selectionMode="multiple"`, and
with a sort descriptor — nothing is thrown. What happens instead: a row without
`isRowHeader` carries `aria-labelledby=""` — **the attribute present and
empty**, which wave 1 refined: the first probe read it with a falsy test and
reported it absent, and a warning written with `hasAttribute` therefore passes
on exactly the table it exists to catch. With selection on, its
checkbox points `aria-labelledby` at an id that **resolves to no element**. So a
table with no row header compiles, renders, types, and hands a screen reader a
column of nameless checkboxes. **That makes it OUR invariant to enforce rather
than one we inherit**, and it is the first thing wave 1 owes.

**A `width` on a `Column` outside a `ResizableTableContainer` silently does
nothing.** Measured: `style.width` is unset and no warning appears, because the
base's own guard is dead code — a `for...in` over an array tests the indices. If
we publish a width, the error that explains it has to be ours.

**The base renders no selection checkbox.** `useTableOptions()` reports the mode
and the behaviour; the checkbox column in the header and the checkbox cell in
each row are written by the consumer of the base — us. The select-all it wires
is named "Select All" from its own bundles, in 34 locales, so that string is not
one we write.

**Sticky survives the resizer, and that tension was imaginary.** Measured in a
browser against the exact inline styles the base merges in
(`table-layout: fixed; width: min-content`): 562px of horizontal scroll moved
the pinned cell **2px**. Pinned columns and column resizing can coexist.

**The other half of the tension is real.** `width: min-content` shrink-wraps:
in a 1400px container a two-column table measured 558px and left **842px
empty**. The table does not fill its container, and that inline style cannot be
overridden by a class.

**And the fix is a different property rather than a fight.** `min-width: 100%`
takes the same table to exactly 1400px. It works precisely because the base sets
`width` and not `min-width`, so our rule wins without an `!important` and
without depending on order. That is the shape to reach for wherever the base
writes an inline style: **beat it with a property it did not set.**

#### What wave 1 found

Six things, and two of them are traps this repository had already written down
and walked into anyway.

**`font-medium` compiles to nothing.** The theme names weights by ROLE, so it
declares `normal` and `strong` and no numeric scale — and a column heading
asking for `font-medium` got no rule at all. That is the **fourth** utility
found this way, after `w-control-md`, `size-box` and `min-w-hit`, and the
lesson is the one already in the package guide: grep the COMPILED stylesheet,
because it is the only place that answers. Every class the component writes is
now checked that way; 44 of 44 produce a rule.

**`outline-hidden` cancelled the focus ring, again.** `ColorSwatchField`
records this in its own source — the utility sets `outline-style: none` and
beats an `outline-2` beside it — and the ring was written that way here
regardless, with the measurement quoted in the comment above it. A browser
check caught it. The answer was already written too: a declared TRANSPARENT
outline is a real outline with a width, a style and a colour, so one
declaration suppresses the browser's default and carries the state where two
cancel.

**The row-header warning cannot be a one-shot effect.** The base renders the
`<table>` element during our render and fills it in a later pass that does not
re-render this component, so when a plain `useEffect` runs, `table.innerHTML`
is the empty string — measured, `tbody=0 tr=0`. A check written that way reads
zero rows and stays silent on exactly the table it exists to catch. A timeout
would "fix" it by measuring the machine (doc 10 §11.2), so what it waits on is
a STATE: a `MutationObserver`, dev-only, answering when rows exist and
disconnecting once it has.

**And `aria-labelledby` is present and EMPTY rather than absent**, which
refines wave 0's finding: that probe read the attribute with a falsy test and
reported it missing. A warning written with `hasAttribute` is satisfied by the
broken case.

**The component's own scroller is a scroll container in both axes.**
`overflow-x: auto` makes `overflow-y` compute to `auto` too, so a
height-constrained table is constrained ON THE ROOT — `className` or `style`,
which doc 02 §6 already sends there. A wrapper with its own `overflow-y`
scrolls the wrapper and leaves the heading stuck to a scroller that never
moved, which looks exactly like sticky being broken and is not. Found by the
sticky check failing on a story that was wrong.

**And a browser check asserted the document does not scroll sideways, which
measured the fixture.** Doc 04 §7's promise is that the page never scrolls
because of US; the catalog's own `.catalog-resizable` decorator is 1274px
inside a 1280px viewport at a 16px offset, so the document overflows by 10px on
every story in the suite. The assertion is now that the component does not
widen the element it was given, which is the half that is ours (doc 10 §11).

#### And then the baselines were opened, which found six more

Every one of these was green: 11 unit tests, 7 browser checks and 7 axe checks.
The pictures found six things anyway, which is the fifth time this layer has
paid for itself in this repository.

**The overflow indication was not visible at all.** The table carried
`bg-surface`, and an opaque child paints over a parent's background image — so
the shadows on the scroller were covered completely and the first baseline
showed a clipped column with nothing marking it. It is the `ColorPicker` lesson
inverted: there a `background-image` painted over its own `background-color`
and the pattern had to move one element OUT; here the shadow was already out
and the opaque child had to stop covering it.

**The cells wrapped instead of scrolling.** Without `whitespace-nowrap` the
browser shrinks columns toward their min-content width, so "Astilleros del Sur"
broke onto two lines in a table that was ALSO scrolling — the crushed-columns
failure, in the component whose whole narrow-screen story is meant to be the
opposite.

**And the shadow, once visible, was too faint to be an indication.** Doc 04 §7
asks that hidden content be INDICATED, and 14% over 12px darkened a body row by
30 levels out of 255, which nobody reads as a shadow. Two things were measured
separately: WHERE the layers land, by painting them in solid lime and red, and
HOW MUCH, by sampling the rendered pixels. The ladder is in `Table.css` with
the value it chose, 24% at 24px, and the cover is a step wider than the shadow
because equal widths leave the leading edge darkened where it should be flat.

**A sample two pixels from the edge measured the BORDER.** With the gradients
removed entirely that column still reads 219 against a mid-row 252, because the
scroller has a border and a radius. Read as a shadow, it said the indication
was present on both edges at all times — a false alarm that cost two rounds
before the control run said otherwise.

**The right-to-left story set the locale and rendered left to right.** Doc 05
§4.1's rule was read as "prefer the locale over `dir`", and what it says is
that the two mechanisms must AGREE: `I18nProvider` tells JAVASCRIPT, and the
stylesheet hears nothing without a `dir`. `Slider`'s own story sets both, which
is what it was measuring. The capture's comment was wrong twice over as well —
it claimed the panel was for the sort mark, which is invisible until a column
is sorted and is a chevron on the block axis, so it does not mirror at all.

**And the panel labelled "A disabled row" had none in it.** The disabled
invoice was the fourth and the story rendered three, so the label described
something not in the picture — the same defect as a `Spinner` panel that said
"inside a filled button" over an outlined one. Worse, the component had no
disabled appearance to show: a state gate box 12 lists by name. The row is
muted now and its hover stops, while the status badge beside it keeps full
strength, because doc 06 §4 rule 7 wants the WHY legible.

#### What wave 2 found

**A static sibling makes the base ignore a render function entirely.** This is
the finding the whole shape of the selection column rests on. The obvious way
to add one is a JSX sibling before `{children}`, and it works for a static
header and silently breaks the dynamic one — measured twice, once with a
hand-built array and once with real JSX children:

```
<TableHeader columns={…}><Column/>{column => <Column/>}</TableHeader>
→ Cell count must match column count. Found 3 cells and 1 columns.
```

The function is dropped and only the static column is built. A data table's
columns come from data, so that is the form that matters, and the selection
column is therefore prepended to the **collection** with the render function
wrapped to recognise a sentinel object. One identity, no magic id a consumer's
own data could collide with.

It also settles why the column is ours to add at all. The base reports the mode
through `useTableOptions()` and leaves both halves — the heading column and the
cell in every row — to the consumer; one written without the other throws on
the cell count, and neither written ships a selectable table with no way to
select anything. §3.4's second measurement said to make that true by
construction, and this is what that costs.

**An empty selection heading is an axe violation.** `empty-table-header`, found
on this component's own story. With several rows choosable the base puts its
select-all box in that cell and names it in 34 locales; with one row at a time
there is no box, because a select-all above single-choice rows offers something
the mode cannot do — so the cell was empty. It carries a visually hidden name
there instead, and the two are never both present.

**A chosen row had no tint, which the baseline showed and no assertion could.**
The checkbox said "chosen" and the row did not, so a selection was something to
count rather than see. The box is the non-colour channel doc 06 §3 asks for, so
the tint is a second channel rather than the only one — and it uses
`--bb-surface-selected` with its paired text colour, because the package guide
allows no standalone "text on a chosen row".

**The union cannot be spread, and this component's stories paid it too.**
Decision 0022 recorded the cost when `ComboBox` met it: `{...args}` plus
`selectionMode="multiple"` has to satisfy the singular branch as well, and it
cannot, because that branch declares the plural props `never`. The decision's
answer is to name a branch; the story took the one arg it wanted instead.

**And the indeterminate dash joined `CheckGlyph` at its second caller**, which
is §8's rule rather than foresight. It was a literal inside `Checkbox` while a
checkbox was the only thing that could be partly chosen; a table's heading box
is the second and means the same thing, so it has to be the same shape. The
cross reached four copies at four stroke weights before anybody noticed.

**The `'all'` sentinel stops at the boundary.** `selectAll()` stores the
literal string, so the first press of the heading box would hand a consumer
typed against a list of ids a string instead. It is expanded here into the rows
that are actually there, disabled ones excluded — the same narrowing decision
0020 does for dates and 0022 for a combo box's keys.

#### What wave 3 found, in its first half

The wave is two PRs rather than one, and the split is along the line P6 already
draws: the arrangement is LOGIC, so it is a hook with tests that render
nothing, and the widths are presentation, so they come with the resizer. Both
land; this records the first.

**There is no column KIND, and that reverses something the analysis proposed.**
The product this suite was read against declares thirteen — `currency`, `date`,
`tags`, `thumbnail`, `stock` — and derives a cell renderer, a skeleton and a
minimum width from each. The analysis read the per-type minimum widths as the
best idea in the component and they are; what was wrong was assuming the TYPE
had to come with them. It is the prop that grows forever, which §7 names as a
warning sign, and it was already broken: four of its own declared types had no
renderer and rendered nothing at all, silently.

So a column declares only what a column can know, and its own type parameter
carries whatever else the project wants — a formatter, an alignment, a text
accessor. The hook passes those through untouched, and what is painted is
composition out of components this library already ships. The minimum width
goes to the second half as a number the column declares, not as a consequence
of a kind.

**And `columns` IS the export shape**, which is doc 01 §4.1 in one line: the
library hands over the visible columns in the order the person arranged them,
and the project performs the act. Because the type parameter is theirs, the
accessor an export needs already travels with it — there is nothing to add.

**A stored arrangement outlives the code that made it, in both directions.** A
column added in a release is in no stored order; one removed is still in an old
one. Both are the normal case rather than an error, so a declared column
missing from the order keeps its declared position and a stored id matching
nothing is dropped. The product this was read from invented an order from array
position with no note on what happens when the two disagree.

**Two things the hook refuses, and both are measured failures.** A LOCKED
column cannot be hidden or moved: it is the one carrying `isRowHeader`, and a
table without it leaves every row named by nothing — wave 0's silent failure
arriving through a different door. And the LAST VISIBLE column cannot go: a
table with no columns is not a narrower table. The product this was read from
prevented that in its dialog and had no route back afterwards, because its
restore was unreachable — so `restore` and `isArranged` are part of the hook
rather than something a consumer is expected to build.

**And the manage-columns panel is the consumer's, demonstrated rather than
asserted.** Non-goal 3 leaves that dialog to them and P6's corollary says an
assembly may have no capability its pieces lack, so the story builds one out of
a `Checkbox` per column and a `Button`. If it had needed anything the hook does
not expose, the hook would be wrong. The baseline is how anybody checks that
without reading the story.

#### What wave 4 found, in its first half

The wave is two PRs. This one is the actions and their fold; pinning the
trailing edge is the second, and it has a design question of its own — how a
CELL learns that its column is pinned, without the same fact living in two
places that can disagree.

**A context provider inside the table reaches nothing inside it.** This is the
finding the whole wave turned on, and it cost three wrong guesses before it was
measured. Written INSIDE `AriaTable`, wrapping its children, a provider reaches
neither a `Column` nor a `Cell` — both read the default. Written above the
table it reaches both.

It is decision 0021's wall in a second shape. There, a filter written inside a
combo box's collection could not see the surrounding context and kept every
option; here, a step written inside the table left every row folded at every
width, on a 760px table with room for all three buttons. The rule that covers
both: **anything a collection's children need to read is provided from OUTSIDE
the collection.**

**And a container query does not apply to the element that declares one.** The
four step classes went on the same element as `@container`, so `--bb-step` read
`base` at every width — the query resolves against the nearest ANCESTOR
container, never against the container itself. Found by a browser probe
reporting `step=base` on a 760px panel.

**So the observed element is a zero-height full-width strip**, which is doc 04
§11.3's own prescription rather than an invention. It cannot be the scroller,
for the reason above; it cannot be the table either, because an observed
element has to CHANGE SIZE with the container and a table inside a scroller is
as wide as its content — `RangeCalendar` measured 408px in a 640px box and
408px in a 320px one, and an observer that never fires reads the step once and
never again. A full-width strip beside the content that is not: `h-0`,
`aria-hidden`, and there to be measured.

**One observation for the whole table, published downward.** A row that
observed its own actions cell would be wrong twice over — the cell is sized by
its contents, and there would be one `ResizeObserver` per row.

**And a destructive row action colours the MENU row and not the button.**
`Button`'s destructive variant is a filled one, which in a row of small ghost
buttons would be the loudest thing on the screen and would read as the row's
primary action rather than its dangerous one. A ghost button that is
destructive is a variant this library does not have, and hard rule 8 wants a
real place that needs it — so it is a §7 row with the case attached rather than
a variant added in passing. Nothing is lost meanwhile: the action carries an
icon and a name, so its danger was never carried by colour alone (doc 06 §3).

**What did NOT need a new rule is the fold itself.** Doc 04 §11.2 — "the '…'
never hides one step" — arrived for the fourth time and needed no new argument:
`Pagination` reached it from the other direction, the collapsed breadcrumb
trail from a third, and a row of actions is the fourth. One action never folds.

#### And what its second half found, which was three things and a correction

The pinned edge is `pinnedEdge="end"`, named for the EDGE because §7 had
already settled that pinning is about a table's two edges rather than a column
somebody points at. That naming is what keeps the fact in one place: CSS's
`:last-child` already knows where the trailing edge is, so a cell never has to
be told that its column is pinned, and hiding the last column pins whichever
one becomes last with nothing to keep in step. The design question the first
half flagged — how a CELL learns its column is pinned — turned out to have the
answer "it does not".

**A transparent pinned cell is a window, and the row's colour is a variable
rather than a class written twice.** Measured on the component: held at the
trailing edge, the cell carried **173 pixels of another column's text inside
it** against 199 of its own. So it has to be opaque — and it also has to wear
whatever its row is wearing, which is a second fact about the same surface. The
row publishes its colour as `--bb-row-bg` and both read it, which is
`internal/seam`'s shape for a divider colour arriving on a background. The row
stays TRANSPARENT at rest, and that is load-bearing: an opaque child paints over
the scroller's background images, which is what the overflow indication is made
of.

**The overflow indication had to move, and moving it made it slightly better.**
An opaque column at the trailing edge covers that edge's shadow completely —
measured, the pinned cell read identically with the gradients on and off — and
doc 04 §7 asks that hidden content be indicated. So the cover and the shadow
both move inward by the pinned column's width and the indication becomes a
shadow the column casts. Sampled in a body row's padding band, where no glyph
can be mistaken for a shadow: **53 levels held against 10 at the end of the
scroll**, where the unshifted edge measures 45 against 10. What crosses from
JavaScript is a WIDTH and never a scroll position, so the covering and
uncovering is still `background-attachment` on a frame the browser chooses.

**A COLLAPSED BORDER DOES NOT TRAVEL WITH A STICKY CELL**, and this one is a
correction of something already written in this wave. Under
`border-collapse: collapse` a border belongs to the table's grid and is painted
where the grid is, so a `border-inline-start` on the pinned cell was left behind
the moment the cell was held: `217,217,224` at its natural place and
`200,200,202` — the shadow, no border — held. The divider is a pseudo-element
now, painted by the cell and travelling with it.

The way that was got wrong is worth more than the fix. **Two probes in this
wave measured nothing, in two different ways.** The first never overflowed at
all — `scrollWidth` 360 against `clientWidth` 360, with `scrollLeft` at zero in
all three of its readings — so it reported sticky working on a table that had
nothing to scroll. The second did overflow and was scrolled to the END, which is
the pinned cell's NATURAL place, where being pinned changes nothing: it reported
the divider present and had only proved that an unmoved cell keeps its border. A
fixture for a pinned column has to overflow AND be somewhere that is not the
end.

**And the wave found a three-pixel border nobody had declared.** Reading a 1px
divider against the row separators is what exposed it: `bb:border-b
bb:border-solid` gives the style utility all four sides, and with no preflight
the three with no declared width keep the browser's initial `medium`. Measured
in paint — `217,217,224` at -2, -1 and 0 — every row separator in every table
in this library was **three pixels**, and the vertical separators between
heading cells were the same accident seen from the side. Nobody had designed
either. It is the package guide's accordion trap, in the one other place a
per-side border is written without an all-sides width; the library was swept and
the table was the only one. Eleven baselines moved and nothing outside the table
did.

#### What wave 5 found, and the foundation it changed

The wave was supposed to be this suite's first caller of doc 04 §6's hook. It
has none. **Rows becoming cards is N2** — a presentational change CSS expresses
completely — and doc 04 §6.3 now says so, with the measurement, because a
foundation is changed before the code that contradicts it rather than
afterwards to justify it.

The whole argument is one reading. With `display` changed on the table, the
rows and the cells and nothing else touched, the roles are identical at both
widths (`grid` / `row` / `rowheader`), the counts are identical (5 rows, 20
cells), the selection survives and the keyboard walks the grid exactly as
before. The base writes its roles explicitly rather than leaning on the tag, so
a `td` that stops laying out as a table cell is still a `gridcell`. §6 rule 4 —
"three rows selected, the table becomes cards, they stay selected" — cannot
fail here, because there is no second structure for state to fall out of.

The test §6.3 leaves behind for the next case is not "does it look different"
but **whether the two layouts can share one tree**. Tabs that become a select
still cannot: the control that disappears owns which tab is chosen. A table's
rows own nothing.

**The label was the wave's real question, and the answer was to read it.** A
card shows "Customer: Marina Quispe", so a heading's text has to reach the
CELL. Every obvious route writes the string twice — a `label` prop repeats it
once per row, a `data-label` attribute repeats it and pollutes the accessible
name through generated content — and decision 0018 already recorded the cost.
So it is read from the column's own collection node, through `columnIndex` in
the base's cell render props and `TableStateContext` above the collection.
Declared once, read three times: the `<th>`, the sort description, the card.

That route is why **the sort-announcement defect had to be fixed first**, in
its own commit. The field it reads was the empty string on every column in this
library.

**A card's title carries no label**, and that is measured rather than tidy. A
row is named by its row-header cell, so a label inside it renamed the row from
`"F001-000412"` to `"NumberF001-000412"` and the row's checkbox to
`"Select NumberF001-000412"` with it. Doc 04 §11.4 calls a name the most basic
thing a component knows.

**And the label is ANNOUNCED rather than hidden**, which is the opposite of
what it looks like it should be. With the heading row gone the accessibility
tree holds **zero** `columnheader` nodes — measured — so nothing else is naming
the field. Above the step the label is `display: none` and the heading does the
naming. Said exactly once either way: `gridcell "Astilleros del Sur"` wide,
`gridcell "Customer Astilleros del Sur"` narrow.

**`display: none` and `sr-only` swap places here**, and the rule that decides
between them is not which hides better. `Steps` uses `sr-only` because
`display: none` took its titles out of the tree and left a list of items with
no names; nothing in `Steps` can be focused. A column header **can** be — the
base's keyboard delegate sends ArrowUp from a top-row cell straight to it — so
a clipped heading is focus nobody can see. Measured, ArrowUp from the first
row: visible → the header; `sr-only` → the header, 73×34 and clipped;
`display: none` → nowhere at all, focus stays on the cell. The deciding
question is whether the hidden thing can be focused.

So the band is EMPTIED rather than hidden: the select-all and any sortable
column stay, because they are things a person can still do, and a table that
neither sorts nor selects loses the band entirely.

**One capability was nearly lost in silence.** Reaching `columnIndex` means
handing the base a render function, which empties that node's `textValue` — the
same trap `Column` had been in for four waves. A row's typeahead string is
built by joining its row-header cells' `textValue`, so typing-to-find would
simply have stopped working with nothing in the console. `Cell` derives the
value back, and the check that guards it types a WHOLE invoice number, because
every row in the fixture begins `F001-0004` and a check written with one
keystroke would pass on a table whose typeahead was dead.

**What the pictures found, which assertions had not.** A grid item stretches,
so a `Badge` in a status field came out as a 180px bar of colour across the
value column. A heading's bottom border, laid out as a chip, came out as a
stray underline stopping partway across the band. And a cell laid out as a grid
makes EVERY child a grid item, so the actions cell put its row of buttons in
the label's column — orphaned at the left under a heading of `VisuallyHidden`
text. The value is wrapped in one element now, `display: contents` at the width
where it is not needed.

**And an empty heading band was still a row.** The band is emptied rather than
hidden, and emptying it by hiding every child left a `role="row"` with no cell
in it — `aria-required-children`, critical, on the three stories with a narrow
panel and nothing to sort or choose. The band itself goes now, matched with
`:has()` so a column hidden through the arrangement hook takes it in the same
frame.

CI found it and a local run had not, which is the part worth keeping. `pnpm
visual` builds the catalog inside Docker and does not refresh the local build
the other suites are served from, so an accessibility run chained behind it read
a catalog two hours old: **497 checks green against CI's 501**. The count
disagreeing with CI's is the tell, and it reproduced on the first try once the
build was current.

**And the wave invalidated four of its own suite's fixtures**, which is the
cost of a boundary at 480px. `TooWide` was a 360px panel and became a card list
with nothing to scroll — the story whose entire purpose is to photograph
horizontal overflow. Two pinned panels and the right-to-left one went the same
way. Widened to 520, and the right-to-left one needed two more columns as well:
at 520 with four it read `518/518`, which is a pinned column at its natural
place, where being pinned changes nothing and the picture proves nothing. That
is the third invalid fixture of this suite and the same one twice.

#### And the narrow structure is doc 04 §6's own first example

That section opens with "a table that becomes a list of cards when narrow", so
the suite is the component that section was written for. Its rule 4 is the one
that breaks most often and it is a check rather than an intention: **if the
person had three rows selected and the table becomes cards, they stay
selected.** The product this was read against has no stacked mode at all — its
only answer to a narrow screen is to scroll sideways — so there is nothing to
carry across here and the rule is the specification.

## 4. The two bottlenecks

**`Field` and the layer base.** More than twenty components depend on them, and
in practice they are irreversible: redesigning them later forces a change to
everything.

They deserve more care than anything else, and it is no coincidence that the
day of experiments with the headless base consists of exactly a dialog with a
dropdown inside and a field with a label, description and error. That day
validates both.

## 6. The risk component

**`ComboBox`, built in the early phase**, even though no project has asked for
it yet.

Reason: it stresses the entire architecture in a single component — field,
layers, keyboard, filtering, locale, long lists — and it is small enough to
redo if it comes out wrong. If the composition model does not hold up to
`ComboBox`, it will not hold up to the table either, and it is far better to
know that then.

The table pieces come afterwards, with the architecture already validated.

## 8. How this is maintained

- **Every finished component** updates its status here, not somewhere else.
- **Every decision to build something** updates this list before the work
  starts, not after. The list is the record of what was chosen.
- **Every rejection** goes in section 7 with its reason, even when it seems
  obvious at the time.
- This document is **not** moved into the foundations: it lives apart and
  changes often.
